Why attention divides by √d

Attention lets each word decide how much to listen to every other word. When the vectors behind it get long, those decisions turn all-or-nothing. Dividing by √dk keeps them soft.

Based on Attention Is All You Need, Vaswani et al., 2017

1From scores to weights

A transformer reads a sentence by letting every word ask a question of every other word. The asking word holds a query vector q, each word it looks at offers a key vector k, and their dot product q·k is a score: how relevant that word looks. Scores can be any real number, so attention turns them into weights with a softmax. The weights are positive and add up to 1.

wᵢ = exp(sᵢ / T) / Σⱼ exp(sⱼ / T)

Each weight w is its score s, divided by the temperature T and exponentiated, as a share of the total.

Below, the word “sat” is asking about the six words of “the cat sat on the mat”. The scores are fixed by hand so you can watch the softmax on its own. The one knob is the temperature T, which divides every score before the softmax. Drag it.

2Sharp or soft is a matter of scale

At T = 1, “cat” gets about half of the attention and the rest is shared. Drag T down to 0.25 and “cat” takes 98%: the entropy, a measure of how spread out the weights are, falls from 2.05 bits to 0.15. Push T up to 5 and the weights flatten toward 1/6 each, close to the 2.58-bit maximum for six words. The order of the scores never changes. Only their scale does, and the scale alone decides whether attention is a spotlight or a floodlight.

The spotlight end is a problem for learning. How much a weight moves when you nudge a score depends on the weights themselves. When one weight is nearly 1 and the rest are nearly 0, a nudge to any score barely moves anything, so a training signal that tries to move attention elsewhere barely registers. A saturated softmax has stopped listening.

3Where the scale comes from

Nobody sets a temperature by hand inside a transformer. The scale of the scores comes from the vectors themselves. A dot product of two dk-dimensional vectors is a sum of dk products, one for each pair of matching components. If every component is random with mean 0 and variance 1, each product also has mean 0 and variance 1, and the variances of independent terms add up. So the score has variance dk and a standard deviation of √dk.

The figure draws one query and eight keys at random, with components from N(0, 1), and shows the softmax of their eight scores with no scaling at all. Drag dk, the width of the vectors, from 1 to 512. The scores spread out roughly as √dk, and a softmax that is soft at dk = 4 turns into a single spike long before 512.

4The fix: divide by √dk

Attention Is All You NeedVaswani, Shazeer, Parmar and 5 others, 2017Open the paper on arXiv suspect exactly this: for large dk the dot products grow large in magnitude and push the softmax into regions where its gradients are extremely small. Their remedy is one division: every score is divided by √dk before the softmax. A score whose spread is √dk, divided by √dk, has a spread of about 1, whatever the width.

Turn on “divide by √dk” in the figure above and drag dk again. The spread now stays near 1 and the weights stay soft at every width. In the paper's base model dk = 64, so without the division the scores would be 8 times too spread out, the same as running the softmax at T = 1/8. Dividing by √dk is the temperature knob from the first figure, set to T = √dk so the scores always arrive at unit scale.

Why the square root and not dk itself? Dividing by dk would shrink the spread to 1/√dk, which is 0.04 at dk = 512, and the weights would be nearly uniform: attention would see nothing. √dk is the one choice that keeps the spread fixed as the width changes.

Takeaway

The sharpness of a softmax depends on the scale of its scores, not just their order. Dot products of dk-dimensional vectors spread out like √dk, so attention divides by √dk: a fixed temperature that hands the softmax unit-scale scores at any width.
