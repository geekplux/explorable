Why coffee cools fast, then slowly

The rate of cooling is proportional to the gap to room temperature. That one rule gives an exponential curve and a fixed half-life for the gap.

Based on Newton's law of cooling

Curated

Heat flows down the gap

A cup at 90 °C in a 20 °C room loses heat quickly. At 25 °C it barely cools. Newton's law of cooling makes this exact: the rate of cooling is proportional to the gap, T − Tₐ, between the cup and the room.

So the hotter the cup is relative to the room, the faster it cools. As it cools, the gap shrinks, and so does the rate. That feedback is the whole story.

The curve

Solve that rule and you get T(t) = Tₐ + (T₀ − Tₐ)·e^(−kt). Drag the point along time and watch the drop slow down. Raise k, the cooling constant, and the whole curve falls faster toward the dotted room-temperature line.

Now watch the labelled marker line while you change T₀. The curve moves; the marker does not. The time for the gap to halve depends on k alone.

A half-life for the gap

The gap halves every ln 2 / k minutes, whatever T₀ is. A cup at 90 °C and one at 50 °C take the same time to halve their gaps: 70 degrees become 35, and 30 become 15.

Drag k along the curve below. A slow cooler, k = 0.01 per minute, needs about 69 minutes per halving; a fast one, k = 0.3, about 2. The curve falls steeply and then flattens, because the half-life is inversely proportional to k.

Takeaway

Cooling is proportional to the gap, so the gap decays exponentially, T(t) = Tₐ + (T₀ − Tₐ)·e^(−kt), and halves every ln 2 / k minutes from any start.
