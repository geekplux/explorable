Noise, one step at a time

An image model learns by watching pictures dissolve into static, one small step at a time, and a shortcut jumps to any step at once.

Based on Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, Ho et al., 2020

Start with data

A diffusion model learns to make data by first learning how it gets destroyed. The destroying half, the forward process, has no learned parts at all: it is a fixed recipe that adds noise. Ho, Jain and Abbeel's denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM) apply it to images, but every pixel follows the same rule, so one number is enough to watch it work.

Our data is 2,000 numbers drawn from two clumps, one around −2 and one around +1.5, each with a spread of 0.25. Think of them as two kinds of picture. The question is what the noise does to the clumps, step by step.

One small step

Each step shrinks the current value a little and adds a little fresh Gaussian noise: xt = √(1 − βt)·xt−1 + √βt·ε, with ε drawn from N(0, 1). The shrink matters. If xt−1 already has variance 1, the new value has variance (1 − βt) + βt = 1. Without the shrink, noise would pile up and the values would drift ever wider.

The step sizes βt follow a schedule. DDPM uses T = 1,000 steps with β rising linearly from 0.0001 at the first step to 0.02 at the last. Early steps barely touch the data; the last steps are 200 times larger.

Jump to any step

Steps compose. Multiply the shrink factors together, ᾱt = (1 − β₁)(1 − β₂)…(1 − βt), and the whole chain from x₀ collapses into one line: xt = √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε. The first coefficient, √ᾱt, is how much of the original signal is left; the second, √(1 − ᾱt), is how much noise has replaced it.

The figure uses that line directly. Scrub t from 0 to 1,000. At t = 100 the clumps are softer but still apart. Around t = 370 they merge into one hump. By t = 600 only 16% of the signal is left and the samples sit on the dotted N(0, 1) curve; at t = 1,000 their spread is 1.01 and the data is gone.

Signal fades, noise rises

Plot the two coefficients against t and the schedule shows its shape. The signal √ᾱt falls slowly at first, then quickly, then hardly at all; the noise √(1 − ᾱt) mirrors it. They cross at t ≈ 260, where ᾱt = 0.5 and the signal-to-noise ratio ᾱt / (1 − ᾱt) is exactly 1, or 0 dB.

Most of the change happens in the first half. Past t ≈ 750 the signal is under 6%, and each further step changes little. Later papers proposed other schedules for exactly this reason: the schedule is a design choice, not a law.

Why the shortcut matters

Generation runs the process backwards: a network learns to predict the noise ε that was mixed into xt, so it can take a little of it away at each step. Training needs examples at random steps, and the closed form makes each one cheap: pick an x₀, pick a t, draw ε and form √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε in one line, with no loop over the earlier steps. DDPM's simplified training objective is just the squared error between the true ε and the predicted one.

Takeaway

The forward process is a fixed schedule of tiny shrink-and-add-noise steps. Their product ᾱt gives a one-line jump to any step, xt = √ᾱt·x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt)·ε, and by t = 1,000 any data has become N(0, 1).
